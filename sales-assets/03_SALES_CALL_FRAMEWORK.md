# The 15-Minute Sales Call Framework

For Liam or Nadav. One page you can keep open during the call.

**The job of this call:** find out whether this contractor is a fit, tell them honestly if they are not, and — if they are — agree a small test with the terms written down.

**The job is not** to convince someone who does not want more work. A contractor with no capacity, no follow-up, and no interest in renovation work will churn and cost you a market.

---

## Time budget

| Minutes | Stage |
|---|---|
| 0:00 – 1:00 | Rapport and frame |
| 1:00 – 7:00 | Discovery |
| 7:00 – 11:00 | Explain the offer and the appointment qualification standard |
| 11:00 – 13:00 | Objections |
| 13:00 – 15:00 | Agree the test and book the next step |

If discovery runs long, that is fine — it is the most valuable part. Cut the explaining, not the listening.

---

## 1. Rapport and frame (1 min)

Do not open with a pitch. Open with why you are calling and how long it will take.

> "Liam from HomeQuote Network. You've got fifteen minutes with me and I'll keep it to that. I mostly want to ask you about your business — what work you want, where you work, and how much you can handle. At the end I'll tell you straight whether your area's available and whether this is a fit. Sound alright?"

**Natural rapport questions:**
- "How long have you been doing pool remodels?"
- "Is it mostly resurfacing, or are you doing full backyard jobs too?"
- "How's the season been — are you chasing work or turning it away right now?"

That last question is discovery disguised as rapport. Listen carefully to the answer.

---

## 2. Discovery (6 min)

Ask these in roughly this order. Write the answers down — you will need them for the proposal.

### Current work sources
- "Where does your work come from right now?"
- "Is that mostly referrals and repeat, or are you paying for anything?"
- "What's working best at the moment?"

### Desired project types
- "Which jobs do you actually want more of?"
- "Anything you'd rather not take?"
- "Are you doing equipment and decking too, or strictly the pool itself?"

### Service areas
- "Which cities do you actually drive to?"
- "Where does it stop being worth the trip?"
- "Anywhere you'd go for a bigger job but not a small one?"

### Average project value
- "What's a typical job worth for you?"
- "And a big one?"

### Minimum acceptable job size
- "What's the smallest job that's still worth sending a crew to?"

### Monthly estimate capacity
- "How many more estimates could your team run this month without dropping the ball?"
- "Who actually runs the estimates — you, or someone else?"

### Calendar and availability
- "Who owns the calendar, and how do you book estimates today?"
- "If I put a homeowner in your diary for Tuesday at ten, what has to happen for you to be there?"
- "How much notice do you need?"

> This matters more than anything else on the call. We are booking into their diary, so if the calendar is chaos the programme fails no matter how good the appointments are.

### Sales and follow-up process
- "What happens after the estimate goes out? Does anything chase it?"
- "How do you know what your close rate is?"

### CRM usage
- "Are you tracking this in anything, or is it phone and notepad?"
- "When we book an appointment, where should it land — a shared calendar, a booking link, or straight into your system?"

### Previous agency or appointment-vendor experience
- "Have you bought leads before?"
- "What went wrong?" *(Let them talk. This is where you learn the objection you will have to handle.)*
- "Did you ever agree in writing what a qualified booked appointment was?" *(Usually: no. That is your opening.)*

### Pricing expectations
- "What were you paying per lead or per appointment before?"
- "What would an appointment need to be worth for this to make sense for you?"

---

## 3. Explain the offer (4 min)

Keep it to four sentences before you stop and check in.

> "Here's how it works. We agree on what a qualified appointment looks like — the project types, the area, the standard. We find the homeowner, we qualify them, and we book them into your calendar for a specific date and time. You turn up, run the estimate, and close the job.
>
> There's no monthly retainer to start. You're paying for the appointment, not the outcome — so if you close it, great, and if you don't, you still paid for the appointment. That's the honest version.
>
> Make sense so far, or do you want me to go back over any of that?"

### Confirming the appointment qualification criteria

Read this out. Do not paraphrase it loosely — this is the thing you are selling.

> "A qualified booked appointment means: a real homeowner or decision-maker, contact details that are valid and reachable, the property's inside the area we agree, they asked about a service that's on your list, they showed genuine project interest, and they agreed to a specific date and time. It's not fake, and it follows the duplicate, cancellation and no-show rules in your agreement.
>
> And to be straight about what it doesn't mean — I'm not promising every homeowner keeps the appointment, or that every appointment becomes an estimate or a signed job. No-shows and reschedules are covered by the replacement terms. Anyone promising you attendance is selling you something I can't."

### What is confirmed before launch

> "Before a single appointment is booked, you'll have in writing: the qualification standard, the price, your area, the project types, how duplicates, cancellations, reschedules and no-shows are handled, how long you've got to dispute one, and what happens if one doesn't meet the standard. You approve it or we don't start."

---

## 4. Objections (2 min)

Handle them, do not argue them. See `04_OBJECTION_HANDLING.md` for the full responses.

The three you will hear most:
1. "We tried buying leads before and they were bad."
2. "Are they exclusive?"
3. "How much per appointment?"

---

## 5. Agree the test and book the next step (2 min)

### Explaining the test

> "I'd rather start you small than oversell you. We agree a set number of appointments — enough for you to judge them against your own numbers, not so many that your diary is buried. You run them, you see what they're worth to you, and then we decide whether to scale, adjust, or stop."

### Agreeing on volume

- "How many estimates could you comfortably run in a month without anything slipping?"
- "Let's start there rather than higher."

> Take the lower number if they hesitate. A contractor whose diary is overwhelmed in week one blames the appointments.

### Closing the call

> "Here's what happens next. I'll put together the test proposal — your area, your project types, the qualification standard, the price, the number of appointments, the no-show and dispute terms. You read it, you tell me what to change, and once you're happy we connect to your calendar and start. Nothing runs before you've approved it.
>
> What's the best email for that?"

Then confirm:
- Email address for the proposal
- Which calendar appointments should be booked into
- Who attends the appointments
- When you will follow up

---

## Disqualify honestly

End the call early, politely, if you hear:

- Cleaning or weekly-maintenance only
- No capacity for more estimates
- No shared calendar, or nobody who reliably attends booked appointments
- They want to pay per closed job only
- They expect guaranteed attendance or sales
- No licence or insurance

> "I'll be straight with you — based on what you've told me, I don't think this is a fit right now, and I'd rather say that than take your money. If [the thing that would change it] changes, call me."

A contractor you turned down honestly will refer you to someone who is a fit.
